The weeks leading up to departure were a strange kind of productive panic. There's a point in any big project where you stop caring about the ideal and start caring about the functional, and July was that point for _Defiant_. Every job that made it onto the final list had to earn its spot with a single question: will this sink us?

**Electrical**

The house bank situation has nagged at me for a while. Solar is great at the dock and at anchor, but motoring into headwinds with no sun is exactly when you need amps and have none coming in. The answer was a Victron DC-DC charger between the starter battery and the house bank — it lets the engine's alternator top off the house bank at a controlled rate without interfering with the [separately isolated power systems](/blog/why-split-power). Getting the right breaker took a few weeks (it showed up mid-June), but once it arrived, the install was clean. Now when we motor, we charge. Simple math.

While I had the electrical panel pulled apart, I dealt with something that had been quietly worrying me: the two main DC bus bars behind the panel sit close enough together that a loose connection underway could be catastrophic. A few minutes with liquid electrical tape — red on positive, black on negative — isn't glamorous work, but it's exactly the kind of thing that turns a "probably fine" into a "definitely fine."

The tank sensor ESP2 also got a proper overhaul. The original wiring had the wrong resistor value on the fuel sender line, and the sensor itself was sitting in the wrong place to make the readings useful. Rewiring meant running power over the long 16/4 cable to a buck converter at the sensor end, dropping from 12v down to 5v right there at the edge instead of running low-voltage signal wire all the way back. Cleaner and more reliable. The hall effect sensor contact issue that had been haunting the water tank readings sorted itself out in the same pass.

**Plumbing**

Cold storage was non-negotiable for an offshore passage. The original icebox conversion gave up the ghost entirely — not repairable, had to be replaced. I waited most of June for Isotherm to come back in stock, which is roughly as fun as it sounds. It arrived in early July and went in without drama.

The electropooper (the marine head, for anyone not familiar with the deeply undignified name) needed a new electrode plate to function. You can hand-pump a marine toilet, but you would prefer not to. New plate in, head operational, dignity mostly preserved.

The cockpit scupper had been draining slowly for long enough that I'd stopped noticing it. Turned out to be a partial clog in the line — nothing fancy to fix once I stopped ignoring it. It drains like a proper drain now.

**Sails and Topsides**

Before I could call the teak done, everything exposed needed a final coat of varnish: the running light bases, the bung plugs on the nameboards. It took most of a morning and several more coats than expected — four more passes once I saw how the first few soaked in — but the wood is protected and looks right.

The dinghy got its chaps sewn. Hypalon is tough stuff but UV and chafe eat it over time, and a well-fitted cover buys years of life. Sewing boat canvas isn't a skill I came into this with, but it turns out a straight line is a straight line whether you're sewing canvas or code.

When I finally closed the hatch the evening before departure, _Defiant_ was as ready as she was going to be. Not perfect — she never is — but ready. Systems checked, storage stowed, tanks full. The word for it in the old logs is "ready in all respects," which I always thought was the kind of phrase that gets said more often than it's really meant. This time I meant it.
