Three months ago we slipped out of Windmill Point at 4am and didn't look back. That story is the last post. What it doesn't cover is everything we've been fixing since.

A shakedown cruise has a specific meaning: it's when you take a newly relaunched boat out and discover what you forgot, what broke during the refit, and what just plain doesn't work anymore. In our case the shakedown list has been long, and deeply satisfying to work through.

**Electrical**

The biggest electrical win of the summer was getting the DC-DC charger wired in between the starter battery and the house bank. The logic is simple: while the engine is running, the alternator charges the starter battery, and the DC-DC charger takes that energy and quietly tops off the house bank. Before this we relied entirely on shore power and the MPPT controller. With the charger online, longer motor-sailing passages keep the bank healthy without us thinking about it.

While the panel was open anyway, we dealt with an issue I'd been nervous about for months. The two main DC buss posts live very close together behind the switchboard, and if one ever worked loose and made contact with the other, the short would be spectacular in all the wrong ways. The fix was two minutes of work—coat each post with appropriately-colored liquid tape—but I'm genuinely relieved to have it done.

The fuel sender circuit got a proper overhaul too. The original setup had a resistor mismatch and too much distance between the ESP2 and the tank senders, which produced erratic readings. The new approach pushes 12v all the way out to the sender edge over the 16/4 cable, steps it down with a small buck converter, and runs the sender contacts directly into the ESP2 out at the edge. Cleaner wiring, better accuracy, and no more guessing what the fuel state actually is.

**Galley and Head**

These are the two systems that make life aboard actually livable.

The icebox situation turned into more of a project than planned. The original goal was to repair the existing conversion, but there wasn't enough conversion left to repair—the whole unit needed replacing. Finding an Isotherm system took longer than expected; nobody had them in stock mid-season. We waited a few weeks before one arrived and got installed. Cold food and cold drinks are not optional when you're offshore.

The electropooper needed a new electrode plate to function. Marine heads do not tolerate being ignored, so this one was handled promptly: new plate in, tested it, done. Exactly the kind of boat job you want—no drama, no surprises.

The cockpit scupper had been draining slowly for weeks, which was annoying but not urgent. Cleared the line and it drains the way it should now.

**The Small Stuff**

The dinghy got a set of sewn chaps to protect the hypalon from UV and abrasion—a project that's been on the list since before the relaunch. The interior wood got a proper wipe-down with Murphy's oil soap, which made the cabin feel noticeably cleaner. A last pass of varnish went on the running light bases and the nameboard bung plugs.

*Defiant* doesn't feel like a project right now. She feels like a boat.
