The weeks before leaving Windmill Point had a particular quality to them — not the frantic chaos of an open-ended refit, but the different kind of pressure that comes from actually needing to finish. Everything on the list was either going to get done or get cut.

Electrical ended up being the biggest bucket. The DC main posts behind the panel had been quietly worrying me for months: two hefty bus bars, positive and negative, sitting close enough together that if one vibrated loose underway the results would be dramatic and not in a good way. A coat of red and black liquid tape over each one, and that anxiety is deferred indefinitely. The Victron DC-DC charger finally went in, wiring the starter battery to the house bank so underway charging no longer depends entirely on the alternator. And the ESP2 — the little microcontroller that reads the fuel level and tank senders — got a complete rewire. I moved it out to the edge where the senders actually live, pushed 12v out over the existing 16/4 cable, and dropped it down to 5v with a buck converter right at the ESP2. As a bonus, the intermittent contact problem I'd been chasing on the hall effect sensor for months quietly went away once everything was co-located.

Cold storage did not go smoothly. What started as a repair call with Louie turned into a diagnosis of full system failure — the icebox conversion needed to be completely replaced, not patched. Nobody had Isotherm units in stock anywhere. I spent a few weeks watching a backorder until the unit finally showed up the first week of July, which was considerably closer to the departure window than I would have liked. It landed, it got installed, it works. There is cold food on this boat.

The electropooper had been sitting useless with a worn-out electrode plate for longer than I care to admit. New plate, back in service, no longer a very expensive piece of fiberglass furniture.

For the teak, I got four coats onto the running light bases and the bung plugs on the nameboards before we left — not perfect, but enough to not feel embarrassed. The full varnish kit is stowed under the quarterberth so I can keep up with it underway. The dinghy chaps took longer to sew than I'm going to put in writing, but hypalon is not something you let slide. UV doesn't forgive, and a good set of chaps buys years.

The cockpit scupper had been draining sluggishly for longer than I noticed it. Cleared the line, and the deck drains properly now.

None of this makes for a great story at the tiki bar. But the boat that motored out of Windmill in July had solid wiring, working refrigeration, a functioning head, and a dinghy that's going to be fine. The work is in her bones.
